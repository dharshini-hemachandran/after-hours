The Visibility Trap

A simulation study of popularity feedback loops, ranking policies and who gets seen.

Open the research reader ↗Source on GitHub ↗
Abstracti

The Visibility Trap asks what happens to who gets seen when a recommendation system ranks content by how much engagement it has already earned.

It is a simulation study, built in Python with Pandas and presented through Streamlit. It compares an engagement-first ranking policy with exploration-based and exposure-aware alternatives on two measures: how evenly exposure is spread among creators, and how much engagement the system still collects.

Simulation, not observationTwo social-media datasets inform the inputs. They are not an audit of any platform's real algorithm.
The questionii

When a recommender ranks by engagement, who stops being seen?

Content that ranks higher is shown more. Content that is shown more earns more engagement. That engagement feeds the next ranking. The loop does not need anyone to intend inequality: it only needs a ranking rule that rewards what has already been rewarded.

Methodiii

Two datasets, three kinds of policy

DatasetPostsColumns
Instagram Analytics Dataset29,99923
Social Media Sponsorship & Engagement Dataset52,21427

The 200 creators with the highest mean views were selected, and each ranking policy ran for 20 rounds. Ranking scores combined normalised engagement, popularity, novelty or an exposure-deficit correction; exposure was assigned by a rank-decay function. A hypothetical sponsorship score combined cumulative exposure, engagement-weighted exposure and follower count. A revised V2 engine adds a visibility-feedback term and is reported separately.

The policies compared

Engagement-first ranks by engagement. Exploration adds novelty, at 35% and 50% weight. Exposure-aware corrects for each creator's exposure deficit, also at 35% and 50%.

Findingsiv

What the simulation shows

Exposure Gini by policy · lower means exposure is spread more evenly

V2 simulation · values from the project's results report

Against engagement-first, the exposure-aware policy at 35% reduced the exposure Gini by 78.9% and the sponsorship Gini by 57.8%, with the engagement proxy changing by −22.7%. Reducing concentration came with a lower engagement proxy: a trade-off, not a free improvement.

SensitivityResults depend strongly on the chosen weights. Near-uniform exposure at high exploration weights should not be read as realistic or optimal.

Limitsv

What this does, and does not, show

It demonstrates

Within the simulation, engagement-first ranking produced greater exposure concentration than several exploration-based and exposure-aware alternatives, and reducing it lowered the engagement proxy.

It does not demonstrate

How any real platform's proprietary algorithm behaves; causal effects of ranking, since both datasets are observational; or actual brand spending and creator income, since sponsorship here is hypothetical.

Further workMultiple random seeds, other creator samples, alternative feedback strengths and explicitly normalised ranking weights.
